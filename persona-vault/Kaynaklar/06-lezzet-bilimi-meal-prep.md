Why do some meals taste better after a few days?

Why does some meals get better with age whilst others just get really nasty?
Let's say our x-axis represents time where zero corresponds to the point at
which a meal is first cooked when it's 0 hours old. Y represents the
enjoyability of a meal. And to normalize, let's say all meals start their life
cycle at y equals 1. So you can look at any vertical deviation as a relative
increase or decrease in enjoyability from when the meal was initially
prepared. Importantly, this isn't to compare enjoyability of different meals,
but instead to compare each against its own baseline. So, if we could somehow
preserve a meal indefinitely, it would track the y equals 1 function. With each
passing hour, it maintains the same level of enjoyability. Now, most meals
track a pretty similar path. They get gradually worse, pass through a barely
edible phase into a violently ill phase before finishing up in the fatality
phase. All meals do eventually end their life cycle at the point at which they
could end your life cycle, but lasagna tracks an unusual path to get there.

Sure, the first few hours as it cools, it might get marginally less enjoyable,
but say you don't finish it all, put some in the fridge and come back a day
later, you're like, is this better? And now it's like here somehow it seems to
have booked the trend and started increasing in enjoyment. and you leave it
another day and a wow what's actually happening here and and you think maybe
this just keeps getting better. So you leave it a week and no no no no that was
stupid. It does eventually get worse. But something curious happened there.
And lasagna isn't unique, but it does seem to belong to a small group of meals
that have this weird Goldilocks phase where for a brief window they actually
improve with age. And if we can figure out why this happens, we can stop
making meal prep that gets intolerable by midweek. We can apply our findings
and actually make meals that are better on the Thursday than they were on the
Monday. Let's take a closer look at the anatomy of a lasagna.

**Acid**, tomato base, citric, and malic wine, if used adds tartaric and
acetic. Together, these tenderize the ground meat, a process that continues
throughout the cool, freeze, thor, and reheat cycle. Acids also balance the
richness you get from meaty and cheesy flavors. And importantly, upon
reheating, they help wake up or brighten flavors that might otherwise have
dulled.

**Starch retrogradation.** When you cook pasta, the starches gelatinize, giving
it a tender, gummy texture. When it cools, starch molecules reassociate and
recristallize into a more ordered structure. That's retrogradation. But when
you reheat once more, it doesn't fully return to its gelatinized state, so it
retains a firmer texture. This is why serving lasagna fresh out the oven gets
kind of messy with layers sliding everywhere and a return trip for all the
bits you dropped. But after cooling and reheating, you can get a nice clean
cut brick. This process occurs with all pasta dishes. The difference is that
typically excess sauce absorption overwhelms the retrogradation so you still
just end up with mush.

The **layered structure and sauciness** help with flavor migration and
melding. Aromatics from the sauce seep into the high surface area of beef and
cheese fats infused downwards. But mainly it's about what this structure
prevents, which is all the damage that usually comes from freezing. We'll
elaborate on this later because it's important and deserves explanation. The
layers and acidity together also help lasagna to get around some rules on
dairy inclusion. See, many forms of dairy are a meal prep no-go because they
can separate, curdle, or become grainy after freezing. But the protective
layers and acidity in a lasagna help stabilize the proteins and fats so they
remulsify smoothly when you reheat instead of breaking apart.

### Golden rules of perfect meal prep

Did you know that there are many other insights we can glean from the anatomy
of a lasagna? But let's distill them all down into some hard and fast rules
that we can apply to meal prep more broadly.

**One, minimize air exposure.** Air exposure, particularly in the freezer, is
catastrophic. We get oxidation causing loss of freshness, faded colors, and
dulled flavor. We get sublimation as low humidity air vaporizes, dehydrating
the surface, creating dry, leathery patches, and just some very strange and
undesirable textures. We get moisture migration and ice crystal growth. TLDR:
water freezes, expands, and the resulting ice crystals rupture cell walls and
membranes, demolishing any semblance of structure and creating mush. We combat
air exposure with appropriate storage, which we'll come to later. But firstly,
with the meal itself, saucy or layered dishes make for the best meal prep
because they provide a protective coating, encasing protein and veg, and
shielding the most vulnerable parts of the meal from all the usual dangers of
air exposure.

**Two, include acidity.** Protein tenderization, flavor brightening, balance,
and melding. Some kind of acidity is key to improvement with age. Tomato based
sauces are the easiest way to achieve this, but not the only way. Think
citrus in curries, balsamic or rice vinegar in glazes and marinades, even
wine, molasses, mustard. We have options and free will.

**Three, favor smaller chunks and higher fat proteins.** Higher fat content
locks in moisture and protects against the drying effect of ice crystals.
Smaller pieces mean more surface area, which is bad when exposed to air, but
which is good when encased in sauces, allowing flavors and acidity to
penetrate. Minced and ground meats incorporated directly into your liquids
work best. But if you do want to prep with larger cuts, like a chicken thigh
for example, fully submerging in your liquids is still going to make a huge
difference versus keeping as a separate meal component.

**Four, separate starchy carbs.** Rice and pasta continue absorbing liquid
during storage and reheating. So even if initially slightly undercooked, you
can often find these turning soft and bloated by the time you consume them.
Keeping them separate prevents this and maintains a far better texture. It's
an extra step, but certainly worthwhile.

**Five, avoid the red list.** Some ingredients simply don't survive freezing
and reheating well for just reasons.

### An example of bad meal prep

Should we ditch the theory and begin some practical? Let's do that. To
illustrate the stark contrast in results when following and not following our
golden rules, here's the worst possible meal prep — not the worst possible
meal, but a meal that's enjoyable when first prepared, but which is an
extremely poor choice for meal prep: a seared chicken breast in a creamy
mushroom sauce with some courgette and spinach, served over white rice and
finished with parsley.

Fresh, it has a nice crust on the chicken breast which is tender and juicy;
courgette that still has structure; a smooth uniform creamy sauce over
perfectly al dente white rice — a rather enjoyable meal. But after 72 hours
and one freeze-thaw cycle, the chicken breast no longer has crisp to its sear,
has become markedly tougher with an uneven texture. Courgette is limp and
soggy. Sauce is no longer very saucy. Rice has gone from al dente to
overcooked. Parsley is unrecognizable — a general smorgasbord of decline.

### An example of good meal prep

Now let's make something that does abide by the golden rules. Chicken thighs
(not breast), cut small, seasoned and browned, removed. Same pan: onion
softened 6–8 minutes, then garlic and ginger 2 minutes. Tomato puree, spices
1 minute. Tinned tomatoes, simmer 5 minutes. Chicken back in. Coconut milk in,
stir, simmer, partially cover 20 minutes. Butter, lemon zest, lemon juice,
taste, adjust. Partially cool in the pan until stirring doesn't release
visible steam.

**Storage and reheating:** Golden rule number one is minimize air exposure,
and the most reliable way is to vacuum pack food — but that's impractical for
sauces (you'd need to part-freeze first, or risk bolognese in your vacuum
packer). The best and easiest solution is to create a barrier with foil on
the surface layer of your liquid: press it down, remove air bubbles, wrap
over the container edges, then add the lid. For extra points, fill containers
with minimal headroom, and make sure everything is fully cooled before it
enters the fridge or freezer.

Golden rule number four is separate your starchy carbs — put rice in separate
containers (no foil needed, but still minimize headroom). Remove curry and
rice from the freezer and place in the fridge the night before eating so they
fully thaw before reheating. When reheating, a pan on the stove is best
practice — it allows more even heating than a microwave, which heats unevenly
and inevitably overcooks some parts.

The result: an example of meal prep that ages well. Rules have exceptions and
can be bent or broken — experiment, but use these guidelines to avoid eating
grim food, because meal prep can be really useful, but life is too precious
and fleeting to spend chewing your way through a freezer-burnt chicken breast
on a Tuesday lunchtime.
