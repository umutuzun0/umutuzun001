**The Science on How to Reframe Anxiety**

I started in graduate school studying emotions, especially anxiety, and figuring out how does it affect different
behaviors like how we negotiate or how we take advice from each other or how we perform when we're public
speaking. One of my biggest findings was how we reframe anxiety as excitement. It's essentially the same emotion
because they're both high arousal, high energy, high cortisol, high heart rate, sweaty palms. You just change how
you think about it in your mind — literally saying things out loud like "I'm excited," which changes your
appraisal of it. So you actually experience excitement. It helps you perform a lot better in a lot of different
ways.

The "nervous Nelly negotiation" study and the "get excited" paper: when we feel anxious, as most people do in
negotiations because it's an intense environment filled with uncertainty and a lack of control (the recipe for
anxiety), we tend to want to escape — either relieve that feeling by making concessions or get out of the
interaction entirely. Reframing anxiety as excitement helps you focus on opportunities rather than threats — how
things could go well rather than poorly — and it has incredible downstream consequences: helps you sing better,
do public speaking better, collaborate more effectively.

In one study, participants were about to sing karaoke in front of an audience. Right before singing, an
experimenter asked how they were feeling, and participants were told to say either "I'm feeling excited" or "I'm
feeling anxious." Just by saying "excited," people sang better — more in tempo, more on pitch, better rhythm, as
measured by software analyzing the actual singing. This doesn't always work — if you're terrified of something
genuinely dreadful (e.g. your mother's terminal illness), you can't just get excited about that. But on the
margin, when torn between nervous and excited, if you can genuinely convince yourself you're excited, the flip,
repeated enough, actually becomes more likely to come true — and this compounds over time when done habitually
before many different high-stakes conversations, not just once.

**If You're Nervous, You're More Likely to Make a Bad Decision**

As a base rate, most people feel anxious before and during a negotiation because it is an intense environment.
Feeling anxious makes people more likely to leave prematurely, make more concessions to relieve the feeling of
anxiety, or make bad offers — none of which helps you if your goal is to claim value.

**Asking for a Salary Raise**

If asking for a raise, you want to go in with as much personal power as possible — e.g., get another job offer
elsewhere first to strengthen your BATNA (best alternative to a negotiated agreement). But the best way to get a
raise, in Allison's personal view (stepping outside her "expert hat"), is to be so valuable that your employer
comes to you wanting to keep you, rather than you having to demand more money. If you prioritize what you can give
rather than what you want, you often end up getting what you want anyway — that's how relationships and
organizations work.

**The Conversational Compass**

Think of a recent conversation more than 5 minutes long. Then ask: why did you want to say what you said? How did
you want the other person to feel during and after? How did you want to feel yourself? How much time did you have?
This reveals the "Conversational Compass": the X-axis is relational (high-relational goals serve the other person
or the relationship; low-relational goals serve yourself), and the Y-axis is informational (high-informational
goals hinge on exchanging accurate information; low-informational goals are not about exchanging information, or
are even about concealing it, or are about protecting time/energy). Each quadrant gets a name:

- High-informational + high-relational = **Connection**
- Low-informational + high-relational = **Savoring** (enjoying being together, small talk that builds warmth)
- Low-informational + low-relational = **Protection** (protecting your time, reputation, secrets — moving quickly)
- High-informational + low-relational = **Discernment / Persuasion** (work-related goals: persuasion, decisions,
  brainstorming)

We're never limited to just one quadrant — we live in all four, and pushing yourself to think about pro-social
goals more often (the right side of the compass) is a virtuous habit, but discernment/protection matters for
relationships too (e.g., keeping a friend's confidence).

**How People Should Really Apologize and When It's Too Much**

The best apologies take ownership without over-explaining or making excuses ("I messed up. I'm sorry.") — not
revisiting the problem in detail or justifying it. The single most effective component of an apology is making a
concrete promise to change ("I'm not going to do this again — here's how I'll be different"). Research on
apologies in normal conversations shows over-apologizing within one conversation (more than twice) starts to feel
like a reminder of the bad thing rather than moving forward. But in a large dataset of parole hearings for serious
crimes, more apologizing (with the concrete change-promise) correlated with actually getting released.

**Never Do This on a Date**

Preparing topics ahead of time — even 10-30 seconds of forethought about what you could ask someone — measurably
improves conversations: less anxiety, smoother flow, fewer disfluencies (ums, stutters), more topics covered, less
likely to blurt things you regret. This doesn't need to be complicated — you can literally jot 2-3 bullet points
in a calendar note before a meeting.

**If You Want to Be Liked, Don't Do This**

An exercise: imagine walking into a room and being told to make people like you a 1/10, 5/10, or 10/10. For 1/10:
ignore them, don't get their name right, interrupt, look at your phone, be arrogant, talk about yourself. For
5/10: engage but blandly, disinterested, small talk only. For 10/10: be fully focused on them, attentive,
sincerely complimentary, get their name right, ask about specifics, crack a genuine joke.

**T-A-L-K Framework** — **T is for Topics.**

Topics are the building blocks of conversation, chosen constantly (not just at the start). This gives you control
to nudge conversations in better directions. Prepping topics ahead of time (even briefly) is one of the biggest,
simplest levers.

**Easy Ways to Have More Casual Conversations / The Topic Pyramid**

Small talk is at the bottom of a "topic pyramid" — it's an important social ritual for strangers or people who
haven't seen each other in a while, marking "okay, we're doing conversation now." The mistake most people make is
staying there too long. As soon as possible (after roughly one beat of small talk), move up toward "tailored talk"
(more personalized, relevant to shared interests) and eventually "deep talk" (topics only these two people, in
this context, could discuss this way) — but you can't skip straight to deep talk either; it usually requires the
ritual of climbing gradually. Asking follow-up questions is the most common way to move up the pyramid quickly.

**The Importance of Asking in Conversations and Dates**

The topline advice: ask more questions. Question-asking sounds simple but is a skill most people underuse. In a
famous study of ~1,000 speed dates with transcripts, the number of questions asked predicted whether the other
person wanted a second date — asking just one extra question per date could convert a first date into a second
date. This effect held for both men and women but was particularly helpful for men, because men on average ask
fewer questions on dates than women. (Also, men are on average more likely to agree to a second date regardless —
"less discerning" in the data). The benefit of asking is driven mostly by great **follow-up** questions, not just
the initial question — it signals genuine interest and arms you with more information to keep exploring.

**Never Do This on a Date (Boomer Asking)**

The common mistake is "boomer asking" (a boomerang, not related to age) — someone shares something vulnerable and
you immediately pivot the conversation back to yourself instead of asking a follow-up question about what they
shared. This makes the other person feel unheard, embarrassed for having shared, and shuts down the conversation.
Even just one good follow-up question before returning focus to yourself can be enough.

**The Meeting Mistake You Shouldn't Make**

In meetings, saying "yeah, yeah, yeah" repeatedly while someone else talks often signals (to an outside observer)
that you're not really listening — you're just waiting to say your own point, trying to talk them into silence.

**Poor Communication Skills Might Be Holding Back Your Career**

Plotting people on an axis of communication self-awareness: some people naturally "get it," others have zero
apparent self-awareness of how they come across — and in some cases this single trait is the dominant gravitational
force on someone's career trajectory, more than talent or hard work. The good news: people with low self-awareness
CAN become more self-aware through structured teaching (e.g., a course), which is often enough to put them on a path
of continued improvement even if they don't transform overnight.

**The Importance of Kindness in Any Conversation** — **K is for Kindness.**

Kindness shows up in small, repeated choices: using someone's name correctly and with appropriate formality;
respectful language. A study of police officers' body-cam footage during traffic stops found that officers used
less respectful language toward Black citizens than white citizens, and that interactions using more respectful
language (regardless of race) went better — fewer conflicts, fewer further infractions. Small choices in the
respectfulness of language have a real, measurable impact, and can carry systemic bias.

Conversation is remarkably effortful — people who know how to be good conversationalists often fall short simply
because they don't have the energy that day (not enough sleep, etc.). Being honest about your capacity in the
moment (e.g. Brené Brown telling her partner "I'm on 10% today") — and giving others that same grace — matters.

**When to Incorporate Levity** — **L is for Levity.**

Levity is humor and warmth, and it exists to combat the much more common enemy of conversation than outright
conflict: boredom and disengagement (the human mind wanders roughly 25% of the time during conversation). Levity
includes: expressing gratitude, giving genuine compliments, shifting topics when one goes stale, and — a favorite
— **callbacks**: referencing something said earlier in the conversation (or even weeks earlier), which shows you
were truly listening and retained it. Callbacks are also a great way to end a conversation gracefully (there's no
universally "right" moment to end a conversation — better to be assertive and exit than to hem and haw).

**The Science Behind Introverts and Extroverts — Is It Real?**

Rather than "introvert vs extrovert" as fixed types, what matters more is personal preference about frequency of
social contact and what topics energize you — and, notably, whether in an awkward silent group moment you feel
compelled to "fix it" (more extroverted tendency) or feel it's not your job to fix it (more introverted tendency).
There's also a distinction between "approachers" who jump in to fix awkwardness (sometimes saying things they
regret), "avoiders" who stay quiet, and people who just want to leave.

**Your Contribution Score in a Conversation**

In group settings, people implicitly track a "contribution score" based on how thoughtful and valuable someone's
past contributions have been — someone who talks a lot but adds little gets discounted before they even finish
speaking; someone who rarely speaks but is consistently insightful gets everyone's attention the moment they start.
Talking isn't the only way to add value in a group — timekeeping, note-taking/diagramming (the person holding the
pen is often powerful), and facilitating/keeping the agenda are all valuable roles.

Bad "switchers" in a group — people who redirect a group conversation back to an unrelated personal topic rather
than reading the room and going where the group is headed — hurt their own contribution score, because it looks
like egocentrism rather than levity (a good levity move briefly detours to something genuinely funny/relevant and
then returns to the group's direction).

**The Importance of Asking / Never Do This on a Date, revisited: 10 Questions to Be Liked**

Ten example questions (based on the "36 Questions to Fall in Love" research by Arthur Aron): What are you excited
about lately? What is something you're good at but don't like doing? What's something you're bad at but love to
do? Is there something you'd like to learn more about / learn how to do? What can we celebrate about you? Has
someone made you laugh recently? What's something cute your kid/friend/pet/partner has been doing? Did you grow up
in a city? Have you fallen in love with any new music, books, movies, shows lately? These are just starting
questions — the real value comes from asking genuine follow-ups about the answers.

**How to Persuade People and Be a Better Salesperson**

Persuasion rarely happens within the bounds of one conversation — it happens through people we trust and like over
time, because we've interacted with them and observed that they're smart, trustworthy, and open to opposing
viewpoints. Ironically, if you push yourself to learn as much as possible about the other person's view and
validate it (even while disagreeing later), you become MORE persuasive over the long term, because they stay
engaged and trust you're reasonable, not a jerk.

**People Confuse Agreement With Listening**

People often conflate "you agree with me" with "you're listening to me" — when someone disagrees, we feel unheard,
even though disagreement and listening are not the same thing. The fix: validate first ("It makes sense that you
feel this way — tell me more about how you got there"), even before you go on to vehemently disagree. Validation
is not the same as agreement.

Andy from the CIA (a former spy) reportedly spent seven weeks doing almost nothing but listening to a target's
taxi driver — asking simple follow-up questions and letting the driver lead the conversation — before, in the
eighth week, making a proposition informed by what he'd learned mattered most to the driver (e.g., his son's
health). Note: interpersonal relationships (romantic partners, coworkers) are more complex than a taxi-driver
relationship because they serve many overlapping roles simultaneously (**multiplexity**) — e.g., a partner is
simultaneously lover, friend, co-parent, roommate — which is harder to navigate than a single-purpose relationship.

Listening is not just passive silence — it has three parts: perception (seeing/hearing everything), processing
(elaborating internally), and — crucially, uniquely possible in conversation — reflecting back what you heard
(paraphrasing, asking follow-up questions, showing curiosity) so the other person KNOWS you heard them. Nodding
and smiling ("active listening 101") don't actually prove you were listening (you could be thinking about your
grocery list) but omitting them is jarring; using your words to reflect content back is "listening 301."

**This Is the Only Conversation That Feels Real**

In a class exercise, students transcribe every incoming/outgoing message (texts, DMs, emails, calls, face-to-face)
over 20-30 minutes of their life. The clear finding: only face-to-face conversations feel "real" in retrospect and
while happening — because our brains evolved for face-to-face interaction. This doesn't make other modes
unimportant (email is great for transactional info), but our conversational lives have become strikingly "unreal,"
correlating with high loneliness.

**What Happens When Replacing Myself With AI**

An experiment: an AI chatbot version of the professor held "office hours" for students. In some ways the AI was
better — always available, not grading the student (removing a conflict of interest that makes students hold
back), and able to generate detailed feedback on conversation quality (topics covered, follow-up questions asked,
levity, listening) at a scale no human professor has time for. But there's ambivalence: a team member's use of AI
to draft interview feedback became noticeable via formatting, and once noticed, made the recipient (Steven) start
ignoring the feedback because he wanted to hear the actual person's judgment, not a bot's — trust is placed in
people, not text. LLMs also tend to push communication toward a "regression to the mean" — flattening out
personality, creativity, and weirdness.

**Show Your Real Self at Work: Yes or No?**

"Strategic authenticity" (a term from NYU's Juliana Pillemer): bringing your FULL, unfiltered self to every
context (e.g., a bachelorette party persona at a work meeting) would be a disaster for you and everyone around
you. Good conversationalists adjust their behavior context to context (like an athlete adjusts) while keeping
their core values consistent. Being "strategically" less than fully sincere in service of a larger sincere goal
(e.g., asking a question you're not dying to hear the answer to, because it serves the conversation's larger goal
of connection) isn't inauthentic — it's living up to who you actually want to be.

**How Culture Around Men Has Shifted / Why Do Men Struggle to Talk About Intimacy? — Male Friendship Study**

In a project observing men meeting for the first time and trying to forge friendships, the professor found it
"maddening to watch" — men would spend many conversations on activities/narration (sports, food, logistics)
without ever asking questions like "what have you been struggling with?" or "what do you hope to achieve?" — the
vulnerability that female friendships often reach within minutes. Men are more likely to bond shoulder-to-shoulder
(doing activities together) than face-to-face (talking directly), and the leap from "activity buddy" to vulnerable
friend can feel too risky, especially for men socialized to see vulnerability as weakness. This connects to
statistics: a large proportion of men report zero close friends; men are far more likely than women to say they
have no one to turn to in a crisis; men's friendship satisfaction has dropped; men's average number of close
friends has dropped sharply since 1990; and men (unlike women) often rely on a romantic partner as their sole
source of emotional support, which is part of why men are much more likely to remarry quickly after a spouse's
death (lacking a friend network to fall back on), while women lean on friends.

The fix: individuals can model the vulnerable leap themselves — asking "What have you been struggling with
recently?" or "What have you been thinking about that you haven't shared with anyone before?" — one conversation
at a time, moving beyond narrating shared activities into something personal.

**How to Teach Your Kids to Speak**

The major long-term project of parenting (18 years) is helping kids learn to talk to other people well — modeled
moment to moment, including navigating failure and difficulty. The professor limits her kids' screen time
significantly (no phone before high school, social media much later) partly because of the risk to attention and
conversational development.

**Closing reflection**

Talk is described as potentially "the advantage that humans have over AI" — the skill that has always mattered
most for achieving everything valuable in life, and increasingly the thing that remains distinctly, irreplaceably
human as AI capabilities grow. The professor's own new "conversation type" quiz (at her website) is designed to
help people identify their own conversational tendencies (e.g., "approacher" vs. "avoider" vs. "exiter" in
awkward silences) and get tailored advice, and there are plans to adapt the TALK curriculum for high schoolers.
