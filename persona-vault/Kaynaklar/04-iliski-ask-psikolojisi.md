Professor Paul Bloom: I'm delighted to introduce the first guest lecturer for this Introduction to Psychology

### Chapter 2. Defining Love and Its Permutations

I'm going to define what love is but then most of the experiments I'll talk about today are really focused more on
attraction than love — who finds each other of romantic interest that might then develop into a love relationship.

Let's start with a definition of love from Robert Sternberg. He has a theory of love that argues that it's made up of
three components: intimacy, passion, and commitment (or decision commitment). He argued that you don't have love if
you don't have all three of these elements.

- **Intimacy** is the feeling of closeness, of connectedness with someone, of bonding — sharing secrets, sharing
  information you don't share with anybody else.
- **Passion** is the drive that leads to romance — physical attraction or sex.
- **Commitment / decision** is the decision that one is in a love relationship, the willingness to label it as such,
  and a commitment to maintain that relationship at least for some period of time.

If you have all three, you have love (Sternberg's "consummate love"). Different combinations produce different
relationship types:

- No elements: **non-love**
- Intimacy only: **liking** (typical casual friendship)
- Passion only: **infatuation** (love at first sight)
- Commitment only: **empty love** (often the final stage of long-term relationships gone bad; also the starting
  point in arranged marriages)
- Intimacy + Passion, no commitment: **romantic love** (Romeo and Juliet when they first met)
- Intimacy + Commitment, no passion: **companionate love** (your best friend)
- Passion + Commitment, no intimacy: **fatuous love** (whirlwind courtship, Hollywood romance, shotgun wedding)
- All three: **consummate love** — complete love

### Chapter 3. The Social Psychology of Love and Attraction

The social psychology of love has really been a social psychology of attraction — what makes people find each other
attractive. Research has focused on seven variables, divided into "the big three" (main effects, obvious) and "the
more interesting four" (subtle):

**The Big Three** (all things being equal):

1. **Proximity** — people in close spatial proximity are more likely to be attracted to each other. Studies in
   Manhattan show a negative correlation between distance (in city blocks) between people's doors and likelihood of
   a romantic relationship. Same effect found on college campuses (distance between dorm room doors).
2. **Similarity** — "Birds of a feather flock together." On any dimension psychologists have measured (height, age,
   attitudes toward capital punishment, sports team preference), more similarity = more attraction. Opposites
   don't really attract; there isn't much evidence for that saying.
3. **Familiarity** — we tend to fall in love with people already in our environment, not a mysterious stranger
   ("some enchanted evening you will see a stranger across a crowded room" is a cultural myth). This works even
   with words in an unknown language flashed too quickly to consciously register — people prefer words they were
   previously exposed to.

**The More Interesting Four:**

1. **Competence, with a caveat (the Pratfall Effect)** — we're more attracted to competent people, but *super*
   competent people can feel threatening. What we're most attracted to is a competent person who occasionally
   blunders. Classic experiment (Elliot Aronson): participants listen to interviews with a "nearly perfect" or
   "mediocre" candidate for a quiz show. The competent person is rated more attractive. Then in half the cases, a
   blunder is added (spilling coffee). The competent person who blunders becomes *even more* attractive
   ("Pratfall Effect") — but the mediocre person who blunders becomes rated as even *more* mediocre. The effect
   only works if you're seen as competent *first*.

2. **Physical attractiveness** — people underreport how much looks matter (surveys say warmth, sensitivity,
   intelligence, compassion, humor matter, and looks don't), but studies of blind dates show that looks predict who
   goes on a second date far better than any of those self-reported values. A study at University of Minnesota
   randomly paired students for a dance; rated attractiveness of the randomly assigned partner predicted whether
   the relationship continued. The "Frizzy Wig" experiment (Aronson): a confederate is made to look attractive or
   unattractive (frumpy clothes, bad makeup, frizzy wig) and gives positive or negative personality feedback to men
   in an interview. When she's attractive, positive feedback delights them and negative feedback really upsets
   them (they'd even seek her out again to try to disprove her assessment). When she's unattractive, the feedback
   barely matters either way. Attractive people's feedback has more psychological impact on us — not fair or
   rational, but empirically true.

3. **Gain/Loss effect** — humans are wired to be more sensitive to change than to steady states. In love, what's
   powerful isn't someone always being positive toward you — it wears out its welcome. What's more powerful is
   someone whose regard for you is *gaining* momentum over time (the "Gain Effect", Aronson) — even if their
   average regard ends up lower than someone who was always highly positive. There's also a "Loss Effect": the
   person who really hurts you isn't the person who was always negative (they can't hurt you anymore) — it's the
   person who was positive and then starts to fade. "You can only hurt the one you love," because you expect
   positive feedback from someone you love, and when it turns negative, it's a blow.

4. **Misattribution of arousal** — you feel physiologically aroused (elevated heart rate, sweaty palms, faster
   breathing) but aren't sure why, so you make up an explanation, and sometimes misattribute it to love/attraction
   for the nearest salient person. Famous field experiment: the "Rickety Bridge" experiment at University of
   British Columbia. Men crossed either a scary rickety rope bridge high above a river, or a solid low bridge. An
   attractive female confederate met them on the other side, asked them to write a story, and gave her phone
   number "for questions." Men who crossed the rickety bridge wrote sexier stories and were more likely to call her
   back — interpreted as misattributed arousal (the fear-induced arousal from the bridge got relabeled as
   attraction to her). Caveat: this study lacks random assignment (adventure-seeking men might simply choose the
   rickety bridge), so it's not a clean experiment — but lab experiments with random assignment (e.g., telling
   participants they're about to receive painful shocks, or having them run on a treadmill before rating an
   experimenter's attractiveness) replicate the misattribution effect even when the source of arousal is fairly
   obvious.

**Serious implication — domestic violence:** People sometimes stay in abusive relationships partly because of
misattributed arousal: "He wouldn't be yelling and screaming at me if he didn't love me" — misattributing anger or
even the fear of aggression as an expression of love or mutual attraction.

### Chapter 5. Question and Answer highlights

- Can someone feel consummate love for more than one person at once? There's a tension between evolutionary
  perspectives (which don't necessarily rule it out) and socially constructed norms (which mostly say no in most
  societies) — the likely answer is "you could, but you probably won't feel un-conflicted about it."
- The "big three" (proximity, familiarity, similarity) are mostly studied for *initial* attraction rather than
  relationship maintenance over time, partly because it's hard to study established couples without interfering.
